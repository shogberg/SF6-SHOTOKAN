## 1. Categorize the player: risk tolerance x temperament
- **Risk tolerance:** how willing they are to pick an option *they understand* is risky. People who don't know an option is risky get miscategorized, so sometimes you have to show them it's risky first (punish it) and watch the option vanish. Risk tolerance stays fairly consistent across the board.
- **Temperament:** impulsive vs mindful/calm. It shifts with pressure and situation. Many players are impulsive on defense (muscle-memory techs and mashes) but mindful on offense, where consequences are visible.
- **Low risk tolerance players** don't pick retaliative options unless pushed. Expect escape options (backdash, jump out, block). If they tech, it's because they haven't yet filed throw as risky. Shimmy once and a low-risk, non-impulsive player usually walls up and blocks. They rarely Level 1 or DP. **Always throw loop low-risk players**; if they finally tech, you've broken their character.
- **Impulsive + low risk:** the easiest to predict. Keep forcing high-pressure situations and they repeat the same decision. Don't question what you know.
- **Impulsive + high risk:** the only type whose defense you must really respect, depending on character. Often you don't need much offense; they'll hurt themselves.
- **Mindful + low risk** (Doc describes himself this way; the student said he's similar): they think about the worst case, so with lethal on the table they may cut the wire early (e.g. wake-up super on the first interaction).
- **Send negative-feedback messages:** punish their favourite option hard (e.g. Doc targets a low-risk Cammy's wake-up dive kick early). Then they panic into something less threatening, and become predictable.
- Character colours it: a low-risk Cammy is more likely to wake-up dive kick than DP.
- Categorize from neutral too, and don't second-guess the read.

## 2. Matchup knowledge: the pop quiz
- You don't need the whole character. You need **the moves you will definitely deal with at every level** (the "adhesive" buttons that hold neutral or start their offense, e.g. Cammy 2HP, Marisa target combo and Gladius, Bison back HK and 5MP, Kimberly's plus pressure buttons, Jamie's spammed HP), their block advantage, and **the top two things players do after them**. Most players do the same things ("most people are cattle"). It's about 5-15 minutes per character.
- For each, **record the two likely follow-ups and build a Venn diagram:**
  - Tier 1: an answer that beats both, safely.
  - Tier 2: an answer that beats one and survives the other.
  - Tier 3: "you're from the future": the worst possible thing you can do to option A (and to B), to take it off the table or to gamble on when you have them in a kill situation.
  - Always weigh the worst case of each.
- Examples Doc gave: Marisa target combo → (uncharged) Gladius → Drive Impact; delay 2LP vs charged Gladius; Kimberly button → run (perfect parry the medium, then decide to parry again or jab); a slower button than jab against stagger/walk-up throws as a hedged trade; vs Jamie standing HP spam, Cammy 2LK then backdash.
- **Know what comes *after* the button,** not just the point of contact.
- Losing a hedged interaction for ~1500 is fine when the reward on the other branch is much higher. Defensively, decide which answer to show (parry, jab, trade button) so you're actually playing the mini-game instead of turning passive.
- Self-rating: score every character /10 on comfort (5 = only a good player beats you, 6 = tournament-ready). Factor in how often you'll see them and whether Cammy already does well there (e.g. Dhalsim lower priority).

## 3. Don't fight the boogeyman
- When structuring offense or defense, **identify exactly which option you're fighting** (which button, at which point). Otherwise your brain tells you everything is coming at once. Specify, specify, specify.
- List the **two primary options** (sometimes three) and build around them; keep "honorary mentions" in a secondary diagram. A rare sweep isn't a surprise; it just wasn't worth thinking about.
- Check whether the thing you fear is even possible (e.g. jumping out after *your own* minus button isn't something they can punish; a poke that can't reach after your spaced string isn't a threat). Turn on the frame meter and test it.

## 4. Offense: defaults that gather information
- **Your default structure is a "perfect boat":** if it works, they never touch you. Anything that breaks it is a hole: easy to spot and easy to adjust for. You only track what's *different* from what you expected ("fool me once... second time is a pattern").
- **Defaults give the least reward**; that's the price of safety. Their job is to tell you what the opponent does, and more importantly what they *don't* do, so you can target the specific option with your biggest punish.
- **When your string is done, your offense is done.** If you reach the end without a hit, exit safely (backdash, a check button that leaves you plus, a spaced trade button) rather than forcing a third button into their counter-poke. Reward and cost sit on one number line: getting back to neutral from "five points in danger" is worth as much as gaining five points.
- **Track where they challenge:** after one jab, after two jabs, after a specific button, on wake-up. Then never give them that third button, change it to one that whiffs their button or pushes you out, or set a frame trap or button that beats their specific press (e.g. Doc found a Cammy string where 2MP trades/beats 2MK and 5HP, and spacing that makes 5LK whiff).
- **Throw is the only option that always gives a definite answer.** Put throw (and shimmy) into your offense. Throw loops are the default anti-defense; use jabs for variance, but the intention is to throw.
- Know what each initiator usually leads to (after a jump, on wake-up, after Drive Rush jab, after forward throw) instead of "they throw a lot". Find one constant and target it; you can unravel a lot from one.
- Drive Rush jab or a meaty 2HP on wake-up chips Drive and makes them respect you; with resources and health, 1 bar for potentially 2 of theirs is a fine trade. Be careful when their character punishes your low Drive hard (e.g. JP with Level 2), and with characters like Lily focus on their weakness, not your strengths.

## 5. Beating fuzzy/delayed jabbers
- Delayed jabs that sit at 5-7 frames are the "golden spot" and too variable to stagger reliably; trying to hit their exact timing just hands them your turn.
- **Change what they have to answer:** delayed jabbers are usually low risk tolerance, waiting to react. Take away their reward and their safety: **just throw them** (or jab then throw; two jabs into throw is one of the worst things for them, especially with no DP). They now have to change their timing or option select, and whatever they change to tells you a lot about them.
- Use a frame trap with a gap of 3 frames or less as an auto-pilot check (it counter-hits immediate presses and trades with very small delays), and use the trade to see what comes out.
- Know which of Cammy's options leave her in or out of throw range (stand LK steps out; 2LK keeps you in after Drive Rush).

## 6. Play Cammy as a scam artist
- Cammy's neutral works *because* people fear the dive kick. That's why she can walk up and jab. Players who press 2MK~Drive Rush or big buttons against her at that range should feel like they're disrespecting her.
- **At her 2MK range she is already a threat**, whether they know it or not; they have to do something. Walking into that range alone puts them in a 50/50 (press and risk the jump, or do nothing and get poked).
- **Dive kicks are a bet on a whiff punish** at least half the time, not just a way in. If they press heavies (Ryu 5HP, JP buttons) when you enter that range, jump or dive kick exactly when they'd press; you delete the button. Delayed dive kicks beat air throws and late anti-airs; aim for their feet.
- Blocked dive kicks at -2/-3 are acceptable for gambling a whiff punish; if they just block, take more liberty on the ground. If they anti-air or block your dive kick, there's always something worse you can do to them.
- When you don't know the answer, **default to Cammy's strength** (jump, dive kick, feints, walk-back dive kick) and let the character carry you while you gather information. Cammy should win where the player doesn't know the answer.
- Know your corner jump-out and anti-air punishes: when you know the jump is coming, kill them for it (Cammy has no DP into Level 3, so learn the max anti-air conversions).

## 7. Cash out on habits
- Reframe recurring opponent habits (jumping after your string, a reversal, a favourite button) as **opportunities to cash out**, not just things to stop. Remove it from the playing field if it threatens your game plan; otherwise save it for when it kills.
- Don't just DP the jump; do the most damaging thing ("I'm trying to end you"), or keep them cornered.
- Your game plan doesn't change; you make small adaptations.
- Late in a round with a lead, you don't need risks: play safe and wait for the inevitable jump/press.

## 8. Training structure
- **50/25/25 split** of each session: half on the primary objective, a quarter each on two secondary ones. Don't sit on one drill longer than about 30 minutes; alternate drills with ranked (drill, ranked, drill, ranked, break, repeat) so it sinks in the same day.
- **Three training partners:**
  1. Someone clearly worse (e.g. a weaker player on a character you dislike): don't drop a single game. It's about subduing their strengths and your weaknesses. Then remove the way you're winning and win with the tool you're working on.
  2. Someone close in skill: force the thing you're working on in sets.
  3. Someone better: don't lose to your known weakness, even if you lose the set.
- Ranked consistency for {{PLAYER_NAME}}: dropping sets to lower-MR players hurts more than results against 1900-2100. Fixing knowledge gaps on uncomfortable matchups (Jamie, Marisa, "yolo" characters) is the low-hanging fruit.
